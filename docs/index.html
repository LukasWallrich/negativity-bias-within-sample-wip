A cautious within-sample comment on negativity asymmetry

Exploratory re-analysis of the Paolini et al. (2024) contact dataset

The short version

The published meta-analysis compares positive- and negative-contact associations largely across different samples. This exploratory re-analysis asks a narrower question: when the same sample contains both contact valences and the same source-coded outcome, is the negative-contact association larger?

In the selected one-pair-per-sample analysis, the answer is not supported. The pooled contrast—Fisher-z(negative-contact r) minus Fisher-z(reversed positive-contact r)—was approximately −0.054 across assumed sampling-error correlations from 0 to 0.90. A positive value would indicate the proposed larger negative-contact association; the estimate instead points slightly in the other direction.

Interpret cautiously. This is an exploratory sensitivity analysis, not a refutation of the published finding. It uses a different estimand, coder-defined outcome matching, a data-reductive pair-selection rule, and an incompletely known dependence structure.

What was compared

The source workbook contains 936 effect-size rows. I retained rows flagged for the authors’ within-subjects analysis, kept congruent positive- and negative-contact effects, and required effects from the same paper, study, sample, and specific source-coded outcome label (v47_IV_Details). Where a sample contained several eligible outcome groups, I selected the pair with the lowest combined coder-defined purity score.

This produced 76 paired samples covering 27,719 reported participants. The participant total is descriptive: the meta-analysis weights effect estimates, not individuals.

For each pair, the contrast was:

Dᵢ = z(rnegative,i) − z(−rpositive,i)

The sign reversal follows the source paper’s convention that positive effects indicate more prejudice. The two correlations share participants and outcome information, but the workbook does not report the covariance needed for exact dependent-correlation inference. The confidence intervals therefore show sensitivity to assumed sampling-error correlations rather than identifying a unique standard error.

A broader row-weighted sensitivity analysis

I also retained all 444 eligible effect rows in 197 matched outcome groups from the same 76 samples. This is an inverse-variance weighted meta-regression with sample-clustered robust standard errors—not a full multilevel likelihood model. Because samples contribute different numbers of outcomes, it is a row-weighted sensitivity analysis rather than an equal-sample replication of the 76-pair estimate.

Its implied contrast was −0.055 (95% CI [−0.098, −0.012], nominal p = .010). The similar point estimate is informative, but the nominal interval should not be treated as definitive: the sampling covariance between paired correlations is not represented in the point-estimate weights, and the robust calculation assumes independent sample clusters. The 76 samples come from only 47 papers, so dependence among samples from the same paper or dataset may make uncertainty understated.

The coarse workbook classification of samples as “classified prejudiced” versus “classified non-prejudiced/egalitarian” did not provide evidence of moderation. The estimated contrast difference was 0.018 (95% CI [−0.084, 0.121], p = .675). This is not a continuous prejudice measure and cannot test the full proposed range-restriction mechanism.

Interpretation

In this exploratory, coder-defined within-sample comparison, the selected contrasts do not reproduce a larger negative-contact association. The estimate is slightly in the opposite direction, but the analysis cannot adjudicate the published between-sample estimand because the estimands, weighting, outcome matching, and dependence structure differ.
  • The highest-purity pair per sample is transparent but exploratory; alternative pairing rules could change the estimate.
  • The specific outcome label is a source coding field, not a guarantee that instruments and analytic specifications are identical.
  • The true covariance between the two within-sample correlations is unknown and may vary across samples; changing the assumed covariance changes both weights and uncertainty.
  • The association estimates are not causal effects and do not estimate a within-person negativity bias.
  • Samples from the same paper or broader dataset may not be independent.

Reproducibility

The pure-pair analysis is in scripts/analyse.js; the row-weighted sensitivity analysis is in scripts/explore_all_multilevel.js. Results are in report/summary.json, report/pairs.json, and report/all_multilevel.json. The public source workbook is available from the authors’ OSF project but is not redistributed here.
